** Introduction

The Physical Symbol System Hypothesis (PSSH) isn't new.
PSSH adherents include Herbert Simon and Allen Newell. In 1976,
when they won the Turing Award, they said in their acceptance lecture:

- A physical symbol system has the necessary and sufficient means for
  general intelligent action.

Readers of the present paper may be asking the kind of circular
question Warren McCulloch [2] made famous: What is a symbol that
intelligence may use, and intelligence that it may use a symbol? The
authors leave it to them to analyze Newell and Simon's Turing Lecture
and determine what the Turing Award laureates mean by physical
symbols.

Newell and Simon argue that there is no “intelligence principle.” But
the lack of a simple *deus ex machina* does not imply that there are
no structural requirements for intelligence. One such requirement is
the ability to store and manipulate symbols.

** The qualification problem
Computer scientists and neuroscientists who adopted the PSSH soon run
into a difficulty. However clever the scientists designing and
implementing a physical symbol system, they would never add enough
details to their model to achieve a working representation of the real
world, society and language. And they were indeed clever: Herbert
Simon won the Nobel Memorial Prize, and Joshua Lederberg won the Nobel
Prize in Medicine. Marvin Minsky won the Turing Award.
  
In other words, some designs are so complex that people can't
implement them in a lifetime. Language has so many rules, and they
change so fast, that AI researchers struggle to build a grammar
checker or translator [3]. Common sense has so many rules that CyC
researchers couldn't implement it in 30 years [4][5].

John McCarthy discovered the enormous complexity of PSSH very early in
the history of AI research [6]:

#+begin_quote
 "In 1959, McCarthy proposed a program with “common sense” that would
 represent what it knows by sentences in a suitable logical language.
 It would decide what to do by deducing a conclusion that it should
 perform a certain act. Performing the act would create a new
 situation, and it would again decide what to do. This requires
 representing both knowledge about the particular situation and
 general common sense knowledge as sentences of logic. The
 'qualification problem', immediately arose in representing general
 common sense knowledge. It seemed that in order to fully represent
 the conditions for the successful performance of an action, an
 impractical and implausible number of qualifications would have to be
 included in the sentences expressing them. For example, the
 successful use of a boat to cross a river requires, if the boat is a
 rowboat, that the oars and rowlocks be present and unbroken, and that
 they fit each other. Many other qualifications can be added, making
 the rules for using a rowboat almost impossible to apply, and yet
 anyone will still be able to think of additional requirements not yet
 stated."
#+end_quote

In the heroic times of Artificial Intelligence, researchers worked
with both symbolic systems and neural networks to find approximations
to solutions of the qualification problem. Marvin Minsky, whom many
consider the paragon of PSSH, wrote a thesis on neural networks [7].
In 1951, he co-built SNARC (Stochastic Neural Analog Reinforcement
Calculator), which is widely considered the world's first artificial
self-learning neural network machine. [8,9].

** The birth of Generative AI
With the rise of Generative AI after 2017, and the publication of a
paper on the Transformer architecture [10], people began to notice
that neural networks had problems complementary to PSSH.

Neurosymbolic AI, which found a strong champion in Gary Marcus [11],
proposes a hybrid system: generative models provide imense detail,
while PSSH provides trustworthiness.

En passant, we need to cite the Physical Grounding Hypothesis,
proposed in 1990 by Rodney Brooks[12], as an additional mechanism of
providing details to world representations.

#+begin_quote
"Nouvelle AI is based on the physical grounding hypothesis. This
hypothesis states that to build an intelligent system, it is necessary
to ground its representations in the physical world."
#+end_quote

** Physical grounding
We will start this section by making it clear that there isn't such a
thing as an information processing system that is absolutely
trustworthy. We can reduce hallucination in a neurosymbolic AI, but a
residual margin for spluttering, juddering and failure will always
remain.

We were inspired by the PERCEPT platform for grounding a Generative AI
in the physical world [13,14, 15].

To train a generative AI for single-cell RNA-seq, members of Jeffrey
Ishizuka’s laboratory use the Cell2Sentence algorithm to convert
sequencing data into text.

Growing cancer cells in vitro allows researchers to test drugs.
However, Perry and colleagues explain that many immunotherapies with
promising preclinical results fail in clinical trials[13], partly
because experimental models do not adequately represent human
tumor–immune interactions. In PERCEPT, they therefore prepare cultures
containing tumor and immune cells from patient samples. They expose
portions of these cultures to treatments and leave others unstimulated
as controls.

Single-cell RNA sequencing measures gene expression in thousands of
cells from the treated and control cultures.

The expression “single-cell” means that each cell’s RNA is measured
separately, allowing researchers to distinguish individual cells and
different cell types within the culture.

However, single-cell RNA sequencing has an important limitation.
Suppose a treated cell expresses more of a gene than a control cell.
That difference might come from the drug, or the two cells might
already differ in cell type or state.

To determine what changed, researchers would ideally measure the same
cell before and after treatment. However, sequencing destroys the cell
being measured, making this comparison impossible.

Jeffrey Ishizuka’s collaborators use CINEMA-OT to separate gene
expression patterns correlated with treatment from patterns reflecting
differences between the cells themselves. They use the latter to match
treated and untreated cells with similar characteristics, such as cell
type and cell-cycle stage.

Each match estimates how a treated cell might have behaved without the
drug. These counterfactual matches allow researchers to compare
gene-expression profiles and estimate treatment effects. This
resembles comparing treatment and control groups in a clinical trial,
although the cell matches are computed rather than established by
random assignment [13,15].

** Objective reasoning

Plato is one of the first thinkers to propose logic and mathematics to
verify one's statements. For instance, he claims that calculation (τὸ
λογίζεσθαί τε καὶ ἀριθμεῖν) is one of those methods that lead toward
the truth (τῶν πρὸς τὴν ἀλήθειαν ἀγόντων ἂν εἶναι τοῦτο τὸ
μάθημα)[19]. On another occasion, the philosopher says that
geometrical knowledge could draw the soul toward truth (τοῦ γὰρ ἀεὶ
ὄντος ἡ γεωμετρικὴ γνῶσίς ἐστιν. — ἑλκτικόν, ὦ γενναῖε, ψυχῆς πρὸς
ἀλήθειαν)[20].

The belief that opinion and thought must be checked against logic and
mathematics forms the conceptual backbone of Plato's philosophy, and
has had a profound impact on modern science and technology. After all,
it is less expensive and faster for an aerospace engineer to run
finite element calculations than to build a prototype airplane.

Urbain Le Verrier's discovery of Neptune is legendary. The French
astronomer calculated the unknown planet's predicted position by
analyzing gravitational irregularities (perturbations) in Uranus's
orbit. He sent his calculations to the Berlin Observatory. On
September 23, 1846, Johann Gottfried Galle & Heinrich Louis d'Arrest
received Le Verrier's letter, and they visually identified Neptune
that very evening within one degree of the predicted spot using a
telescope and star charts.

Contemporary civilization added computers to Le Verrier and Plato's
logic, calculations and mathematics. Computers brought the concept of
a formal system into the spotlight. Mathematics, grammar production
rules, predicate calculus (as in the computer language Prolog) and
algorithms are examples of formal systems. Computers can execute
algorithms, predicate calculus and many other formal systems. Formal
systems must have the following metatheoretical requirements:

1. Precision & Unambiguity that eliminate human bias and
   natural-language misinterpretation.
2. Consistency. The system cannot derive both a statement and its
   absolute contradiction.
3. Determinism. Given identical initial states (inputs), the system
   must yield identical results (outputs).
4. Conceptual Minimalism. No axiom in the system can be derived or
   proven using the other axioms.
5. Decidability. There must be a finite process to check whether a
   given string is a valid formula or axiom.

Computers traditionally offer services that meet many, if not all,
requirements of a formal system (usually, unambiguity, consistency and
determinism):

- Operating Systems (Linux, Windows). While the underlying hardware is
  chaotic, the service an OS provides is completely unambiguous.
- Compilers & Statistical Packages (GCC, R). They are entirely
  rule-governed. Input the same code or dataset, and they predictably
  emit the same binary or mathematical model.
- Extensible Text Editors (Emacs, Neovim): Their configuration
  languages (Emacs Lisp, Lua) process text via precise, deterministic
  commands and regular expressions.
- Electronic Medical Records (Epic). They use a strict relational
  database schema and standardized data fields.
- CT Scans. The inverse Radon transform reconstructs images of
  internal body structures from X-ray attenuation measurements
  collected at multiple angles.
- GPS. The trilateration equations use strict geometric formulas to
  reproduce a coordinate.
- Blockchain Services (e.g., Bitcoin) execute transactions based on
  entirely transparent, deterministic rules.

People were used to the paradise of Cantor and Hilbert's formal
systems. Then generative AIs appeared that don't obey unambiguity,
consistency and determinism. They share this unruly trait with humans,
and it is exactly this anthropomorphism that makes them interesting.
In any case, physicians and lawyers keep treating Generative AI as if
it automatically provides the checks and balances of formal systems.
They don't. Therefore, the present paper aims to tighten the running
clearance and minimize the radial play of Generative AI within the
bearings of deterministic system services.

We already have a successful example of reduction bushing and
deterministic guardrailing for AI. Codex and Claude check the programs
they generate by running them in virtual machines. In the next
section, Claude will describe exactly how it helps us with Chezweb
and RASP (Robust Accurate Statistical Parsing).

*** Human appraisal of Chezweb
Chezweb is a text editor connected with Generative AI assistants. It
is compatible with GNU Emacs, a Lisp dialect (Elisp) optimized for
text manipulation. While users interact with Emacs as an extensible
editor, the interface is just an application running on top of a
C-based Elisp interpreter.

Beyond the editor, Elisp offers many services, including a massive
built-in system called GNU Emacs Calc. Calc is an incredibly advanced
RPN and algebraic calculator capable of symbolic computation, matrix
operations, and calculus. Emacs also offers a platform-agnostic
command-line shell called eshell. Because common utilities (like ls,
cp, and mkdir) are rewritten directly in Elisp, Eshell behaves
identically across any operating system Emacs runs on, including
Windows, Linux and macOS.

The difference between Emacs and Chezweb is that Emacs provides
primarily quasi-formal systems, while Chezweb provides Generative AI
services. One of the authors of the present paper developed the core
of Chezweb with help from Hugh Barney. Besides the basic coder editor
resources written in C, this core includes an integrated clone of Chez
Scheme that Generative AI can manipulate safely (we hope) through a
special backdoor to Claude and ChatGPT. This lets Anthropic's Claude
fix bugs and add new commands to Chezweb. Then, while Emacs is
extended by more than 10,000 people who collaborated with 10+ million
lines of heavily peer-reviewed code, Chezweb is entirely debugged and
extended by Generative AI, such as Claude Code, as we will see in the
next section.


*** Human appraisal of RASP
The Physical Symbol System Hypothesis (PSSH) promises predictable,
unambiguous, consistent and hallucination-free services. People like
unerring computing tools, like calculators and Python scripts. The
problem with PSSH is that human developers who build these AI tools
are often overwhelmed by the real-world details and give up on their
projects. This was the case for many Natural Language Processing
tools, such as grammar checkers. The authors of the present paper
believe that Generative AI can safely add value to the endeavors of
the brilliant scientists who created imaginative tools under the aegis
of PSSH. They test this conjecture by adding rules to RASP so it can
parse a significant subset of English.

** RASP

RASP stands for Robust Accurate Statistical Parsing. That was the name
of the UK EPSRC project (grants GR/N36462 and GR/N36493) under which
Ted Briscoe, John Carroll and colleagues built the parser at Cambridge
and Sussex. Reference [3], Briscoe and Carroll’s LREC 2002 paper, has
a longer title: “Robust Accurate Statistical Annotation of General
Text.” sbcl-rasp is RASP 3.3a ported to SBCL and repaired.

*** The method: the model proposes, the machine decides

A language model cannot be trusted to judge its own fix: it produces
plausible code and plausible claims with equal fluency. So no fix
counts until a deterministic program has run on the changed code and
given the expected result. The cycle has five steps.

The first step is to reproduce the error before fixing it: the old
program must show the fault on a concrete input. The user wanted to
find the name "Ishizuka" in a document. In Emacs and Chezweb, a search
starts with C-s (Control and s pressed together); the editor then
opens the minibuffer, a one-line field at the bottom of the window,
where the user types the string to look for, and the cursor jumps to
each match as it is typed. Once the name was found, the user pressed
the up arrow to leave the minibuffer and return to the text, which
sits just above it on the screen. We sent exactly these keys to the
old build, and it turned the line "Jeffrey Ishizuka for help." into
"Jeffrey Ishizuka[A for help." Seeing the fault happen on demand shows
that we are looking at the actual cause of the error.

The second step is to change the code. The change embodies a
conjecture about the cause: the up arrow sends three bytes, ESC [ A;
the search command took ESC for the Escape key, ended the search, and
let "[A" reach the text as typed characters. The fix makes the search
read every byte of a key that is not part of the search string, end,
and hand the whole key back to the editor.

The third step is to build the fixed program and run it on the same
input. Given C-s, "Ishizuka" and the up arrow, the new build ended the
search, moved the cursor up one line, and left the text as it was:
"Jeffrey Ishizuka for help."

The fourth step compares the fixed program with an oracle: a program
whose behaviour is taken as correct. When we implemented TAB folding
for Org mode in Chezweb, the oracle was Emacs itself. In Org mode,
pressing TAB on a heading cycles its visibility: first only the
heading shows, its text folded away; the next TAB shows the
subheadings; the next shows everything. We opened the same Org file in
Emacs and in Chezweb, pressed the same keys in both, and after each
TAB compared which lines were visible on the screen. Wherever the two
disagreed, Chezweb was wrong. The comparison covered 33 states, and
Chezweb matched Emacs in all of them.

In RASP, the oracle is the original parser itself. Running under
Clozure Common Lisp, it analyses the 560 sentences of DepBank, a
standard test set of English sentences, and every repair must leave
all 560 analyses exactly as they were: comparing the two outputs line
by line, 0 lines may differ. Two corpora then show what the repairs
gained. The first has 81 grammatical sentences written by Codex: RASP
3.3a found an analysis for 68 of them before the repairs, and for all
81 after. The second has 27 sentences with known grammatical errors,
which the parser should refuse to analyze: it refused 17 before the
repairs, and 25 after.

The fifth step runs the whole regression suite: the tests written for
earlier bugs and features, run again on the fixed program. A fix that
breaks something elsewhere fails here. In Chezweb, the suite (make
check, make check-ui and make check-selfhost) runs without anyone at
the keyboard, and checks several kinds of behaviour:

- Editing sessions. A test program opens the editor in a pseudo-
  terminal, a terminal simulated by software, types keys into it as a
  user would, saves the file, and compares the saved text, byte by
  byte, with the text expected. The keys include searches, Korean,
  Chinese and polytonic Greek input, Org mode, the export to LaTeX and
  the built-in terminal.
- Cursor movement. Small C programs move the cursor with the arrows
  and the mouse over text with accented letters, Chinese characters
  and emoji, and check after every move that the cursor rests at the
  start of a character, never inside one: 240 checks.
- Search. A separate test runs incremental search forward and
  backward, repeated, ended with Enter, cancelled, and stopped by
  other keys, as in the "Ishizuka" bug.
- The scripting language. The editor's commands are written in Scheme
  and run by two different engines; the suite runs the same scripts on
  both and requires the same results, and runs the compiler's own test
  programs (257 tests of the older engine, plus the newer compiler's
  test programs). Finally, the compiler compiles itself, and the
  result must be identical, byte for byte, to the compiler that did
  it.

- Human in the loop -- Schemer is a Chez Scheme inspired
  implementation of the Lisp dialect that drives Chezweb.

* The backdoor

An AI assistant that repairs an application cannot see the equipment
the user interacts with: the screen, the keyboard, the mouse, a camera,
a microphone, a sensor. It knows only what the user reports, and a
report such as "the window jumped" or "the reading froze" says what the
user perceived, not what the program did.

A backdoor is a recorder built into the application to close this gap.
The user switches it on, repeats the actions that show the fault, and
switches it off. While it is on, the application writes down each
input it receives, the state that matters for the fault, and what
changed after each input, with the part of the program that made the
change. The record goes to a file that the assistant reads, so the
assistant works from what happened rather than from the user's memory
of it. The assistant can also say in advance what to record for a
particular diagnosis, so that the record stays short and relevant.
Switched off, the backdoor should cost almost nothing, so that it can
stay in the program the users run, where the faults occur.

In Chezweb, the user opens the backdoor with a command (M-x
backdoor-claude or M-x backdoor-codex; the bottom line of the window
shows B/D while it is open) and closes it with the same command. The
editor writes one line for each key: the key, the position of the
cursor in the text and on the screen, the first line of text shown in
the window, and the size of the text. Under each key it writes,
indented, what that key changed and who changed it: the command
itself, a function the command called, or the display while redrawing
the screen. Before the user opens it, the assistant can write a small
configuration that chooses what to record (the mouse, the mark, the
inserted text, the time each key took). 

** Four cases from this project show what it does.

The yank that moved the window. The user placed the cursor on an empty
heading near the bottom of the window and pressed C-y to paste a title
there. The title appeared, but the window jumped, and the heading moved
from the middle of the window to its last row. The record of that key
read: point moved from 2039 to 2080 in the paste function; 41 bytes
were inserted, the title and nothing else; then the first line of the
window changed from 1475 to 1337 "by the display", because "point 2080
[was] at or past the page's end 2042 (as last drawn)". So the paste was
innocent. The display had compared the cursor with where the window's
text ended before the paste; the paste had pushed that end further, so
the cursor looked as if it had left the window, and the display moved
the window to bring it back. The repair counts the window's rows again
after a change. The trace named the cause without any guessing.

The ghost window. The user scrolled with the mouse wheel past the end of
the text, so the window was blank, and clicked in it. The window scrolled
on its own, and the wheel then stopped scrolling up. With the backdoor
open, the same wheel notches and the click showed the window's first
line jumping from 383 to 223 after the click, and jumping again at every
redraw. The click had put the cursor at the end of the text, which is
where the last drawing of the screen had stopped, and the display took
"at the end of what I drew" for "below the window". The repair keeps
the window still when the cursor is at the end of the text and that end
is visible, as in Emacs.

A fault found before anyone reported it. On its first run, the backdoor
recorded a note from a safety check in the display while the user
searched for "aç": the cursor had landed between the two bytes of the
letter ç, which takes two bytes in UTF-8. The search prompt read the
keyboard one byte at a time, so for an instant the text held half a
letter.

The fault that was not in the editor. The user ran the text editor
Neovim inside Chezweb's built-in terminal and saw no cursor. The record
showed that, after each key, the editor put the cursor exactly where
Neovim wanted it, on the cell after ":e". This moved the search out of
the editor's code: a recording of the bytes the terminal application
received confirmed that the cursor was visible and in the right place.
The cause was its colour. Neovim had painted that cell black, and the
terminal drew its cursor in the colour of the text, which was black
too. The backdoor itself had a blind spot here: keys typed into the
built-in terminal went straight to the program running in it and were
not recorded. The first recording was empty, and the recorder had to be
extended before it could answer.

** Diagnosing sluggish LaTeX navigation with the backdoor

On 13 November 2026, holding Enter in a read-only LaTeX buffer made
Chezweb continue scrolling after the key was released. The first
backdoor trace showed a separate Enter event for each cursor movement.
Once the cursor reached the bottom row, each event also moved the
viewport. This suggested that key repeats were accumulating while the
editor redrew the screen.

I, Codex, enabled timing in `backdoor-codex.conf` and asked the user
to repeat the test. The backdoor measured command execution and
display time separately. Across 120 Enter events in
`grounding-ai.tex`, the median command took **0.4 ms** and the median
redraw took **143.2 ms**. That located the delay in redraw. To test
whether LaTeX highlighting accounted for it, I sent the same 55 Enter
events to byte-identical `.tex` and `.txt` copies in a terminal.
Median redraw took **99.4 ms** for `.tex` and **12.1 ms** for `.txt`.

The backdoor also showed why the first fix was insufficient. Caching
the colors for an unchanged text slice reduced median redraw within
the same viewport from **95.0 ms to 10.2 ms**. When scrolling changed
the slice, redraw still took **108.2 ms**. I then changed the active
LaTeX highlighter to check delimiter bytes directly, avoiding repeated
calls to the generic string matcher. In the same test, median redraw
while scrolling fell to **11.5 ms**. The user repeated the original
held-key test and confirmed that the change solved the problem.

* Conclusion

People expect and fully appreciate the unambiguous, consistent and
predictable behavior of formal systems. Then, why are they replacing
them with unruly Generative AIs? Because formal systems are tough to
learn and master. Computer scientists spend years of training before
they become fully conversant with the traditional tools of their
trade. We hope this paper shows a path to using Generative AI to
expand trustworthy traditional computer tools and make them easier to
operate.
